01

How public sector cases work

Deloitte's Government and Public Services practice, GPS, works with federal, state, and local governments and public institutions like universities. The big difference from a commercial case: the client isn't trying to maximize profit. It's trying to deliver its mission within a budget.

Public prep guides describe GPS first rounds as a behavioral section plus a case of about 30 minutes, and final rounds as longer and more open, sometimes with a group case. Recommendations should weigh mission impact, funding limits, and stakeholder buy-in.

Two sides of a budget

Where the money can come from

  • The operating budget: what comes in and goes out each year. Tuition, state funding, salaries, utilities.
  • The balance sheet: what the university owes and owns. Debt, promises to retirees, buildings that need repair, land, and cash reserves.
  • Most candidates only cut the operating budget. Managing the balance sheet can free up money every year without touching jobs.

Adapt your frameworks

Commercial → public sector

  • Profit becomes mission outcomes within a budget. Here: students who stay and graduate.
  • Layoffs carry public costs: severance, lost services, union rules, and attention from the legislature.
  • Stakeholders matter more: trustees, staff, faculty, students, and the state.

Twelve terms to drop naturally

Structural deficit

A gap that comes back every year because costs grow faster than revenue. One-time fixes don't solve it.

Recurring vs. one-time

A recurring saving repeats every year. A one-time gain, like selling land, helps only once.

Reduction in force

A formal layoff of positions, usually with notice periods, severance, and union rules on who goes first.

Attrition

People leaving on their own, by retiring or moving on. Leaving some of those jobs open shrinks staff without layoffs.

Voluntary early retirement

Offering eligible staff a payment to retire early. It costs money once and saves every year after.

Refinancing

Replacing old debt with new debt at a lower interest rate, like refinancing a mortgage.

Callable bond

A bond the borrower is allowed to pay off early. That's what makes refinancing possible.

Retiree health liability

Health benefits promised to retirees, called OPEB: other post-employment benefits. Large and often overlooked.

Deferred maintenance

Repairs a campus has put off. It's a hidden liability: the bill grows the longer it waits.

Energy savings contract

A company pays for building upgrades and is repaid from the energy they save, so no cash is needed up front.

Days cash on hand

Cash reserves divided by daily spending. How long the university could run with no new money. Lenders watch it closely.

Discount rate

The share of sticker tuition given back as the university's own aid. A 34 percent discount on 24 thousand dollars means students pay about 15,840.

The case prompt

Interviewer

Ridgeline State University is a public university with about 12 thousand students, 2,600 employees, and a 400 million dollar budget. Last year it ran a 24 million dollar deficit, the third in a row.

The board's finance committee has proposed cutting 240 staff positions, about 9 percent of the workforce, to close the gap. The president wants to avoid layoffs if she can. She asks us: can Ridgeline balance its budget without layoffs, and if not, how few can it get away with? The state requires a balanced budget within two years.

Your move

Restate the question in one sentence before you do anything else.

Strong answer

"So the question is whether Ridgeline can close a recurring 24 million dollar gap within two years without the 240 layoffs, and if some cuts are still needed, how to keep them to a minimum, all while protecting students. Is that right?"

Interviewer: "Yes." Saying "recurring" shows you know a structural gap needs savings that repeat every year, not one-time fixes.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. In a public sector case, ask about constraints and stakeholders, not just numbers.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • Is this a one-time gap or a structural one?
    Structural. The deficit grew from 8 million to 16 million to 24 million over the last three years. Enrollment has now leveled off, so finance expects it to hold near 24 million.
  • Besides the yearly budget, what does the university owe and own: debt, reserves, other obligations?
    Good question. We'll show you. The board's rule is to keep at least 60 days of cash on hand.
  • What would the layoffs involve?
    Staff only, not tenured faculty. The union contract requires 3 months of severance and cuts by seniority. About a third would come from advising, financial aid, and student services.
  • What's off the table?
    Sticker tuition can rise no more than 3 percent a year, and state funding will stay flat.

The second question is the one most candidates skip. A budget gap isn't only a spending problem: debt, retiree promises, land, and reserves can all create room.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

How would you structure the problem?

Your move

Take 60 seconds. Diagnose the gap, test the layoff plan, find alternatives, and cover timing and risk.

Strong framework

Can Ridgeline close a $24M recurring gap in 2 years without 240 layoffs?
1Diagnose
  • Revenue and cost drivers
  • Structural or one-time
  • How fast it's growing
2True cost of layoffs
  • Gross vs. net savings
  • Severance and one-time costs
  • Lost services and tuition
3Alternatives
  • Balance sheet: debt, retiree health, buildings, land
  • Workforce without layoffs
  • New revenue
4Bridge and risk
  • Reserves as a bridge
  • The 60-day cash floor
  • Triggers and a backup plan

Say it like this

"I'd look at four things. First, what's driving the gap, and whether it's structural. Second, the true cost of the layoff plan, because the headline savings usually overstate what you actually get. Third, alternatives in three buckets: the balance sheet, like debt and retiree benefits; the workforce without layoffs, like attrition and early retirement; and new revenue. Fourth, timing: alternatives take time to ramp up, so how we bridge with reserves and what would trigger a backup plan. I'd start with the drivers."

Why it works

  • It treats layoffs as one option to test, not the default.
  • It adds the balance sheet, which most candidates miss.
  • It plans for timing and risk, which public boards care about most.

Your move

Size each item as a yearly saving. Watch for the one that's a one-time gain dressed up as a fix.

Hint: recurring or one time?

Ask of every item: does this save money every year, or only once? Only recurring savings close a structural gap. One-time money, like reserves or a land sale, can only buy time.

  1. 1 · Refinance bonds$200M × (6% − 4%) · $2M cost pays back in 6 months$4M
  2. 2 · Retiree health$12M × 25%$3M
  3. 3 · Energy contractNo upfront cash · $30M off the backlog$2.5M
  4. 4 · Lease the landLease, don't sell: a sale helps only once$1.5M
  5. 5 · Balance sheet total$4M + $3M + $2.5M + $1.5M · 46% of the gap$11M

Quick check: a trustee says, "Selling the land brings in 20 million. That covers most of the gap." What do you say?

The single most important idea in this case: one-time money can't fix a structural gap. It can only buy time while recurring fixes ramp up.

08

Close the gap without layoffs

Interviewer

HR has also sized workforce savings that don't need layoffs. About 200 people leave Ridgeline each year on their own, and 80 staff say they'd take an early retirement offer of 6 months' pay. Add two revenue ideas, and here's the full menu. Does it close the gap within two years?

Your move

Add up the three buckets and compare with 24 million. Then check timing: the state wants balance in two years, and these don't all start on day one.

Hint: plans ramp up, and rarely land at 100 percent

The finance team expects about 13.2 million in year 1, 24.2 million in year 2, and the full amount in year 3. Year 1's shortfall, plus one-time costs, has to come from reserves, which can't drop below 60 days of spending.

  1. 1 · Balance sheet$4M + $3M + $2.5M + $1.5M$11M
  2. 2 · Workforce, no layoffs$6M + $4M + $3M$13M
  3. 3 · Revenue$3.2M + $2.8M$6M
  4. 4 · Total by year 3$11M + $13M + $6M vs. $24M125%
  5. 5 · At 80% delivery$30M × 80%$24M
  6. 6 · TimingYear 1: $13.2M, $10.8M short · Year 2: $24.2M, balanced with little roomOn time
  7. 7 · The bridge$10.8M + $4M + $2M from reserves → $73M ≈ 67 days≈$16.8M

Quick check: why not just spend the 24 million of reserves above the floor to cover one year, and decide later?

Spending reserves without a recurring plan just delays the layoffs a year, with thinner savings left to fall back on and a weaker credit rating.

09

Sequence the levers

Interviewer

The president can't launch everything at once. Score these six options, including the layoffs, for ease and impact. Which three go first?

Your move

Score each with a one-line reason, then name your first three.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "I'd start with three that save money fast and protect jobs. First, attrition: about 200 people leave each year on their own, so leaving 1 in 10 of those jobs open saves 6 million by year 3. Second, refinancing the callable bonds: it's a financial transaction, not a people decision, and it's worth 4 million a year within months. Third, the voluntary early retirement offer, which saves 4 million a year and lets people choose. Layoffs score high on impact but low on ease, so they're the backup, not the first move."

    The recommendation

    Interviewer

    The president presents to the board next week, and the finance committee still favors layoffs. What's the recommendation?

    Your move

    Lead with the answer. Then the plan, why it beats the layoffs, the bridge, and the backup.

    Strong answer

    AnswerPlanWhy not layoffsBridgeBackup

    "Ridgeline doesn't need the 240 layoffs. It can balance its budget within the state's two-year deadline with a 30 million dollar plan that protects jobs: about 11 million from managing the balance sheet, 13 million from workforce savings without layoffs, and 6 million of new revenue."

    "The layoffs look like 24 million, but after severance, unemployment costs, and lost tuition, they save only about 14.6 million in year 1 and 22 million after that. Instead, we refinance the callable bonds, move retirees 65 and older to a group Medicare Advantage plan, sign an energy savings contract, and lease the surplus land. On the workforce side, we leave about 1 in 10 vacancies open and offer voluntary early retirement."

    "We bridge year 1 with about 17 million from reserves, which keeps cash at about 67 days, above the board's 60-day floor. The risk is delivery: year 2 balances with almost no room, and below 80 percent delivery the plan falls short for good. So we set a trigger: if savings are more than 10 percent behind plan at month 12, we make targeted cuts in low-enrollment areas, sized to the shortfall, instead of 240 across the board. And we protect advising and financial aid, because they keep students enrolled."

    Bonus drills: public finance math

    Quick reps

    1. A college refinances 80 million dollars of bonds from 5.5 percent to 4 percent. What's the yearly interest saving?

    "1.2 million dollars. 80 million times 1.5 percent."

    2. A city spends 730 million dollars a year and has 150 million in reserves. How many days of cash is that?

    "75 days. 730 divided by 365 is 2 million a day, and 150 divided by 2 is 75."

    3. An agency lays off 50 people who cost 90 thousand dollars each, with 4 months of severance. What does it save in year 1?

    "3 million. 4.5 million of pay saved, minus 1.5 million of severance: 50 times 30 thousand."

    4. An early retirement offer pays 6 months' salary to staff earning 80 thousand. 30 accept, and 20 of their jobs aren't refilled. What's the one-time cost, the yearly saving, and the payback?

    "1.2 million once, 30 times 40 thousand. It saves 1.6 million a year, 20 times 80 thousand. Payback is 9 months."

    5. In a group case, a faculty member says refinancing is "just accounting." How do you respond?

    "It's real cash: 4 million less in interest every year. That's the same as 40 jobs at 100 thousand dollars, saved without a single layoff."
